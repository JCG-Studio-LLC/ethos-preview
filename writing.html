Meta Cognition

October 2026

Why is therapy helpful? At the very basic level, therapy helps a person put their emotions into words which are listened to and validated by the therapist. There is science to back why this is helpful.

Strong emotions are experienced in the limbic part of the brain. This is a rudimentary automatic response. “Thinking about feelings” or using metacognition, moves the emotions to parts of the brain where they can now make sense.

Journaling also works to help process emotions as it is also meta cognition, or thinking about feelings. Just naming emotions brings a certain level of relief.

Where does therapy come in? Cognitive behavior therapy helps one name the emotions and validate them. It helps one identify and correct additional narratives or behaviors that add to a person’s distress. It is a top down approach which helps many people feel better.

Trauma therapy is needed basically when cognitive behavior isn’t enough.

ACT and The Happiness Trap

November 2022

I have been interested in learning more about Acceptance and Commitment Therapy for some time. I used to be a CBT therapist using solution-focused therapies and cognitive or top down strategies. Then I took a course to become certified in trauma treatment and became highly interested and knowledgeable about neuropsychology. I began using mindfulness and meditation personally and in therapy along with many other bottom up or somatic interventions. I bought a year subscription to the Calm app, began meditating, and listened to all the master classes offered. Then I took a free course in Mindful Self Compassion after being introduced to it by my own therapist. It fit so beautifully with all my theories.

Recently, I took the first module of my husband’s life coaching class which is basically intensive CBT. Interestingly, they added values exercises which I found extremely personally helpful, particularly but not exclusively, in doing shame work. Then I read a book recommended by my daughter, Bittersweet by Susan Cain. I also listened to a podcast the author did with Brené Brown. This resonated with all the work I do around emotions. This book suggested ACT.

I immediately ordered three books by the ACT giants. So far, The Happiness Trap by Russ Harris is my favorite. I am going to recommend it to every client.

ACT talks about everything I understand about emotions, accepting them, non judgement, sitting in them… and adds mindfulness and meditation in a very practical way. Additionally, it adds values exercises. ACT seems to take all of my philosophies and approaches and puts them into easy to understand and use explanations and exercises. Mind blown.

ACT starts with the basic understanding and acceptance that life is hard and beautiful at the same time. It challenges the culture that says the goal is happiness, or getting rid of emotions such as anxiety or grief. It proposes that we observe our emotions and life experiences and notice our thoughts and try to unhook from them. Through a variety of strategies and practices, we learn to make choices that move us toward our values and who we want to be, by unhooking from unhelpful thoughts and behaviors.

I’m super excited to learn more, apply it personally and add it to my repertoire with clients. I highly recommend this book.

How to Break Up with Your Therapist

January 2022

The therapeutic relationship is a unique one. One sided, it is all about the client, their feelings, their story, their needs. Though therapists use some self disclosure, the focus is on the client. But that doesn’t mean that therapists don’t have feelings. Many clients, unable or unwilling to express their desire to end therapy, just stop showing up. Though professionals, therapists are people too.

Ideally, the end of therapy is discussed starting in the first session. Sometimes clients seek out therapy for specific, well defined reasons, to work through a life circumstance or relationship, or to find relief from specific symptoms. The therapist and the client together write goals. When those goals are achieved, therapy is done.

But it isn’t always as clear as that. Often, there are underlying issues, trauma or unresolved childhood or other issues that are uncovered in therapy. New treatment goals are then created or current ones rewritten. Ideally, these are conversations that the client and therapist have together.

But what if you are just done with therapy? Perhaps it costs too much or is too painful during a particular time in your life. Maybe therapy wasn’t what you expected or you aren’t pleased with the direction that therapy is going, or you don’t feel a fit with the therapist… Whatever the reason you don’t want to continue in therapy, I promise that the therapist wants to have that conversation.

Therapists are used to clients ending therapy. Ideally, this is anticipated, discussed, even celebrated together. Sort of like a graduation. Therapists and clients discuss the progress, the growth, some of the lessons learned. It is a transition. It is imperative that this conversation happens, for both the client and for the therapist.

So what do you say to your therapist if you think you want to stop therapy? Use the direct approach. “I am thinking that I want to stop therapy and I wanted to talk to you about it.”

Please, don’t just no show. Don’t just stop coming, leaving your therapist hanging. Even if you aren’t happy with your therapy, your therapist would much rather hear that than nothing at all.

Coping

March 2021

Over the years of doing therapy, I have learned so many coping skills. There are strategies for overall self care and wellness. There are emergency calm strategies. There are specific activities to create specific chemical responses. These strategies are important and effective. Even as a person seeks assistance through medications and therapy for depression, anxiety, trauma, they can employ these strategies alongside. They are good for everyone, not just those suffering from mental health issues.

Dopamine is the reward chemical. You get it when you’ve done a good job or accomplished something. Making to do lists and crossing items off gives you dopamine. Some other ways to increase dopamine are creativity, drinking coffee, green tea, human contact, eating, getting a massage, music, self care activities, sunlight, and sleep.

Serotonin is a calming chemical. Eating complex carbs, cycling, essential oils, happy memories, meditation, running, probiotics, therapy, and visualizing are ways to increase serotonin.

Endorphins are feel good chemicals we get from exercise. Changing temperatures, dark chocolate, giving gifts, laughter, kindness, and sitting in a sauna also can increase endorphins.

Oxytocin is the love chemical. Affection, acupuncture, compliments, discovering new things, Lovingkindness meditations, volunteering, working together, time with friends and social media can increase oxytocin levels.

There are also coping strategies that target specific trauma reactions. Accepting our emotions helps with all of the trauma reactions of fight, flight, freeze and fawn. Breathing, grounding, rocking in a rocking chair, safe connections and connecting with the self help with the fight response. Pets, safe places, touch, grounding, self compassion and weighted blankets help with flight and freeze. Boundaries, safe connections, self awareness, and more help with fawning.

There are so many other strategies for overall wellness that help us cope. Increasing water intake, getting enough sleep, eating well, vitamins, gratitude, prayer, changing our mindset, comedy, conversation, decluttering, going for walks, hot baths, pets, mindfulness, resting, sex, visualizing, yoga, journalling, worship, and so much more, help us manage the many emotions that life creates.

If you aren’t sure what works for you, try them! I recommend checking in with yourself regularly and doing something that helps physically, mentally, emotionally, relationally and spiritually every day.

Self Compassion

March 2021

I have recently taken a number of credit hours in learning mindful self compassion. All of us have an inner dialogue whether we are aware of it or not. Many times, it is a critical voice, a voice of perfection or shame, or just a general feeling of not enough. We tend to ruminate on difficult experiences such as a conflict or an endless to do list. Recognizing this inner dialogue is the first step. Changing it is a next step but it isn’t as easy as thinking positive thoughts. I have found using self compassion steps to help us manage difficult emotions and silence that inner critic is helpful.

There are three models of self compassion steps that I use. The first, created by Kristin Neff, has three steps. First, we name, validate and allow our emotions. So many come to therapy for these steps. Having a compassionate listener validate our feelings is so beneficial. However, we can learn to do this ourselves. It’s crucial to learn to allow our feelings without judging, controlling, “shoulding” or shaming them. This is also a difficult yet important step. Step two is common humanity, a reminder that we aren’t alone. Of course we feel that way, who wouldn’t? Often we feel so alone with our feelings, and truly believe we are the only ones who feel that way, or that others will see us differently if we share them. That is another important benefit to therapy, but we can also remind ourselves that emotions are common, no one is perfect or has a perfect life or handles everything that comes our way.

Step three is to nurture our emotions, to ask what we need. This is the most difficult step. We are so hard on ourselves. We expect things from ourselves and say things to ourselves that we would never ever say to a friend. In therapy, to teach this step, I often have a client bring to mind a person that they care very much for and then put that imaginary person in the chair in the office. I have the client imagine this loved one suffering in the same way they are suffering. Then I ask what the person needs them to do or say. Often they will say, “I’d tell them it’s going to be ok, it’s not their fault. I’d give them a hug.” Almost all clients agree this is pretty much the opposite of what they say to themselves.

A second model of self compassion I learned from Tara Brach has four steps. RAIN. Recognize, Allow, Investigate and Nurture. It leaves out the common humanity piece but follows the same basic steps as described above.

The third model is the simplest with only two steps. Step one, knowing everything you know about yourself and all that you’ve been through, does what you are feeling make sense? Step two, ask yourself, what do I need? A hug? Reassurance? A boundary? A rest?

Many clients I talk to feel that self compassion is self indulgent but it is quite the opposite. We must be kind to ourselves before we can ever be genuinely compassionate with others.

Emotions

March 2021

Emotions. Such tricky things. We feel overwhelmed by them, driven by them. Or we detach and avoid them completely. Sometimes they happen as a result of experiences. Often they just happen with no explanation. We can’t think them away. Good thoughts and positive thinking help but that alone is not the answer.

Often, we have a feeling, which is basically a chemical reaction in our brains and then we have thoughts about that feeling that cause us more feelings and more thoughts. We can get in feeling loops, anger, shame, rumination, anxiety. People talk about racing thoughts, all consuming thoughts, inability to control thoughts.

I have several analogies I use to help gain perspective on emotions. One is of the ocean. The ocean has moods. Some days are calm, some are active, and some are completely rough, even dangerous. We can’t control the ocean. We can notice its moods and respond to it in helpful ways. When playing in the ocean, it is imperative to be aware of the waves, to turn toward them, observe them with curiosity, prepare for them. But they can’t be stopped. We can dig our feet into the sand and resist, causing the wave to break over us, but we risk being drowned. We can ignore them, risking them breaking over us and beating us down. Or we can turn toward the waves, jump a little or even dive into them and let them carry us gently to shore.

Another helpful analogy is to think of a creek. After a rain, the creek can become very full, the water rushing over the rocks. We can easily get swept away, feeling ourselves beat against the rocks, carried downstream helplessly. We can try to dam up the water to no avail. Or we can get onto the bank and watch the water go by, with curiosity. Our thoughts can become like leaves we toss in and watch float away.

One of the benefits that clients seek when receiving therapy is to get help in naming their feelings. In addition, I help clients notice emotions in their bodies, for example, tense necks, hot faces, fullness in the chest. Clients can then treat their feelings with curiosity and learn to manage them through a variety of strategies. Clients gain perspective about their feelings, manage instead of ignore or try to stop them.

Fawn

October 2020

I recently learned of a 4th trauma response. When overwhelmed with terror, humans have automatic responses. Fight, flight, freeze and a 4th, fawn.

A fawn response is similar to Stockholm syndrome. It is keeping the abuser or danger close in order to be safe. For example, responding to threats like a dog rolling over on its back wagging tail and hoping for mercy and a scrap, loyal even to the worst “master”. People develop this in childhood as a response to being attacked or shamed for expressing any self interest or self expression. Key signs include:

  • Feeling small or helpless in relation to others.
  • Looking to others for how to feel in a relationship or situation.
  • Difficulty identifying feelings, even when alone.
  • Often feeling one has no identity.
  • Constantly trying to please others.
  • First instinct in a conflict is to appease the angry person.
  • Ignoring own beliefs, thoughts, and truths and accepting those of the people around you.
  • Experiencing unusual emotional responses when issues do not involve people of importance, emotional outbursts at strangers or sudden sadness throughout the day.

Treating those with fawn responses includes helping clients learn strategies to create and maintain boundaries, to talk about feelings and emotions, and to learn to interact with others without the constant need to please, helping the client develop self protective skills, and grieving the pain and loss of being without self interest. Clients must develop assertiveness skills and become aware of and able to stay present with fears that evoke the fawn response.

Those with whom I have shared the insight of a 4th trauma response have found such relief to be able to name their pain and explain automatic responses as adaptive at the time they were learned. Recognizing and unlearning such responses creates freedom and strength.
